## 2 Months

By 2 months, most children: calm down when spoken to or picked up, look at a
parent's face, seem happy to see a parent approach, smile when talked to or
smiled at, coo or make gurgling sounds, turn head toward sounds, watch a person
move, hold head up when on tummy, and move both arms and both legs.

## 6 Months

By 6 months, most children: know familiar people, look at themselves in a
mirror, laugh, blow "raspberries," take turns making sounds with a caregiver,
make squealing noises, hold their head steady without support when sitting,
lean on hands to support themselves when sitting, roll from tummy to back, push
up with straight arms when on tummy, and reach out for objects.

## 1 Year

By 1 year, most children: play games such as pat-a-cake, wave bye-bye, call a
parent "mama" or "dada" or another special name, understand "no," put something
in a container and take it out, look for objects that are hidden, pull up to
stand and walk holding onto furniture, and drink from a cup without a lid,
briefly, without help.

## 2 Years

By 2 years, most children: notice when others are hurt or upset, point to
things in a book when asked, say at least two words together such as "more
milk," name items in a picture book, run, kick a ball, and eat with a spoon.

## 3 Years

By 3 years, most children: notice other children and join them to play, show
concern when a friend is crying, string together words of three or more, use
words like "I," "me," "we," draw a circle when shown how, avoid touching hot
objects when warned, climb well, and walk up and down stairs holding the rail.

## 4 Years

By 4 years, most children: pretend to be something else during play, ask to go
play with other children if none are around, say sentences of four or more
words, answer simple "who," "what," "where" questions, name a few colors,
catch a large ball most of the time, and pour water with adult supervision.

## 5 Years

By 5 years, most children: follow rules or take turns when playing games with
other children, sing, dance, or act for others, use or recognize simple rhymes,
count to 10, name some numbers between 1 and 5 when pointed to, hop on one
foot, and use a fork and spoon.

## When to Seek Advice

A caregiver should talk with a doctor if a child is not meeting milestones for
their age, loses skills they once had, or shows any of the following at any
age: not responding to their name, not making eye contact, not smiling or
showing warm, joyful expressions, or losing previously acquired language or
social skills. Early identification and referral to early intervention services
improves long-term outcomes.
